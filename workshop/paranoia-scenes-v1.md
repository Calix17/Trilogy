# PARANOIA SCENE KIT — Book I, Part One (drafts, not yet canon)

*Six candidate scenes to raise dread and danger earlier in Book I, each built on the
book's core rule: dangerous AND deniable. Every scare must survive two readings —
"something real and threatening is happening" and "a grieving man is coming apart." Nothing
here confirms which. Draft only; choose later; then fold the winners into the manuscript.*

---

## 1. THE LEAK (nightmare that bleeds into the day)

He was in the cellar, and the walls were breathing.

Not metaphorically — the plaster swelled and sank, slow, like a chest, and his father stood
with one palm flat against it, listening, the way he always stood. *You hear it,* Daniel said,
without turning. *You've always heard it. You just call it something else.* And under the
breathing there was a sound, far down, the drag and hush of water on shingle. The sea, where no
sea could be. His father took his hand off the wall and there was a mark there, pressed into the
plaster, and the mark was the tree from the notebook, and the small dot of the fruit was
glowing, and it was a phone screen, and it was ringing —

Elias woke with his heart going. 4:11. Lena asleep beside him, the room ordinary and grey.

Just a dream. He knew the shape of his own dreams and this was one, dressed up in the day's
worries. He lay back down.

In the morning there was a voicemail on his phone from a number with no name, left at 4:11.
He played it. Eleven seconds of nothing — a hiss, a room tone, and under it, if he turned it
up, if he wanted to hear it, the drag and hush of something like water on shingle.

He told himself it was a pocket-dial. A dead line. The sound of any empty room. He did not
tell himself how a call at 4:11 had put the sea in his dream, or the dream the sea in his ear,
because the order of those two things was a door he was not going to open before coffee.

## 2. THE CAR (midnight, the trash, the figure)

The kitchen bin was full and it was his to take out, so at eleven-forty he carried it down the
drive in his slippers, the night cold and clean, and that was when he saw the car.

Across the street, two houses down, under the gap between the streetlights where the dark
pooled. A sedan, dark, he couldn't say the color at that hour. Someone in the driver's seat.
Not doing anything. Not on a phone, no glow. Just sitting, the way you sit when you are
waiting for something to come out of a house.

He stood with the bag in his hand and made himself look. A plate — he could get half of it,
the first three characters, if he — the figure's head turned, slowly, toward him.

He put the bag in the bin. He did not run, because running was what a crazy person would do,
and he was cataloguing, now, at all hours, which things a crazy person would do. He walked back
up the drive at a normal pace and did not look again until the door was shut, and when he looked
through the glass the space under the streetlights was empty. No car. No figure.

Which proved it was nothing. A neighbor. A man on a phone call he didn't want to take inside.
Gone now, because people who are nothing simply leave.

He checked the lock twice. He had not checked a lock twice in years. He noted that too, and
added it to the list, and the list was getting long enough that keeping it was itself on the list.

## 3. THE SONG (Sami, the line, the hands)

It was a nothing evening. Sami was in the bath, and Elias was on the floor of the hall sorting
the recycling, half-listening to his son sing the tuneless, self-forgetting song of a child who
believes he's alone.

*...you don't have to carry it, don't have to carry it, don't have to carry it aloooone...*

Elias went still.

"Sami." He was up, in the bathroom doorway, and his voice was wrong and he heard it be wrong.
"What did you just sing."

Sami looked up from the water, already knowing from his father's face that something had gone
bad and not knowing what. "I don't — nothing. A song."

"Where did you hear it." He was crouched at the tub now, and his hand had closed around his
son's thin wet arm, and he did not remember deciding to take hold of it. "Sami. Where did you
learn those words."

"I don't know!" The boy's face crumpled. "Off — off a thing, I don't know, off your phone,
you had it — Dad, you're hurting—"

"Elias." Lena in the doorway. The particular stillness of a woman looking at something she
doesn't recognize. "Let go of him."

He looked down at his own hand on his son's arm. He let go. Sami pulled his knees up and
wouldn't look at him, and Lena came in past Elias without quite turning her back to him, the way
you keep a careful angle to a thing you're not sure of, and lifted the boy out.

Later she asked him, gently, evenly, in the voice you'd use on a ledge, whether he'd been
sleeping. He said the words had upset him and he couldn't explain why and he was sorry, so sorry,
and all of that was true. He did not say that the crisis line ran an ad with that exact line, or
that a ten-year-old should not have it by heart, because he had heard, tonight, standing in his
own bathroom, what it sounded like from the outside. It sounded like a man losing his grip on a
child in the bath over a song.

Sami, when he checked later, was asleep, or pretending. On the boy's tablet the history was
ordinary. Cartoons. A game. Nothing with the line in it. Which meant Sami had it from somewhere
the tablet didn't show, and Elias stood in the dark hallway and could not make that fact lie
down.

## 4. THE LOOP (the watchers, feeding it) — texture, threaded through several scenes

It became a machine that ran on itself.

He was, he knew, not quite right lately — sleeping badly, checking locks, going quiet in the
middle of sentences — and the people around him had started to notice, and the noticing had a
texture he could feel, a half-second too long before someone answered him, a brightness in the
voice you use on the unwell. Mara at work, twice now, had asked if he was okay in a way that
wasn't really a question. His sister called Lena instead of him.

And here was the trap, closing: the more they watched, the more watched he felt, and the more
watched he felt, the more the ads seemed aimed, and the more aimed the ads seemed, the more he
went quiet in the middle of sentences — which was the thing that had made them start watching.
He could see the loop from outside and could not step out of it, because seeing it from outside
was, itself, the kind of thing they were watching for.

The worst part was the small cold voice that said: what if they're right? What if the reason it
all feels aimed at you is the simplest one — that there is something wrong with the instrument
doing the feeling, and the instrument is you. The ads only upset him now because he was already
turning; a well man would have swiped them away. Their concern wasn't causing his fear. It was
confirming it.

## 5. THE RUN (deniable pursuit — hold for late in Part One)

He'd parked three streets from the house because the spot outside was taken, and the walk back
was nothing, a walk he'd done a hundred times, except that tonight there were footsteps behind
him and they kept his pace.

He told himself: a man going home. The street goes where the street goes; someone walking on it
is not following you. He slowed to test it. The footsteps slowed. That's confirmation bias, he
told himself — you slowed and listened harder, that's all. He sped up. The footsteps sped up, or
the blood was up in his ears and he was hearing his own pulse and calling it feet.

Then he ran.

And the footsteps broke into a run behind him — that he was sure of, or as sure as a man can be
of anything with his heart in his throat and his own shoes loud on the pavement — and he ran the
last two streets like a boy, graceless, terrified, and hit his own gate and spun with his back
to it, ready, chest heaving —

Empty street. A jogger, far back, in reflective stripes, turning off at the corner without a
glance. Or no one. The streetlights, the parked cars, the ordinary night.

A jogger. It had been a jogger the whole time, running because joggers run, and he had sprinted
home from a man out for exercise and would now stand shaking at his own gate like — he made
himself finish the sentence — like his father. He went inside. He did not turn on the lights for
a while. He could not have said, if Lena had asked, and she didn't, whether he'd been chased or
had simply, finally, started to run from nothing at all.

## 6. THE DOOR (the woman, the noise)

There was a woman who sat by the entrance to the parking structure, most days, with a paper cup,
and most days he gave her what coins were in the door tray, a transaction so worn it needed no
faces. Today he had a note and gave her that instead, because he was rattled and being kind to a
stranger was a way to feel like a man who was fine.

She took it, and looked up at him — really up, into his face, which she never did — and said,
clearly, in a voice with nothing wrong in it at all:

"He found the door. You know. Your father." A small nod, almost kind. "He found it."

The world went very thin. "What did you say?"

And she was just an old woman with a cup, smiling now, the moment already gone from her face.
"Thank you," she said. "Thank you very much, God bless." And went back to watching the middle
distance, the way she watched it every day.

He stood there. A bus went past and blew grit up off the street. Had she — had she said his
father, or had he built that out of a hiss of traffic and a "thank you very much" and the thing
that was already living in his own head, the way you hear your name in a running tap? He almost
asked a third time. He saw himself doing it — a shaking man interrogating a woman with a paper
cup about a father he'd told no one he was looking for — and he didn't. He walked into the dark
of the structure, and his own footsteps came back off the concrete, and under them, or not, was
a sound like the drag and hush of water on shingle.
