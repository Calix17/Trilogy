# Supplied feedback — Fermi, LUCA, and ancestral seeding

24 September 2026. Feedback quoted by the user. Preserved as supplied; its claims and proposed story additions are assessed in the accompanying review, not automatically adopted as canon.

## First feedback — verbatim

"Yes, assuming you mean the Fermi paradox, it's the same idea at a larger scale, and that's what makes the whole thing hang together.
The logic runs straight through:
For a society: bounded, resilient, low-consumption systems last longer than growth-at-all-costs ones.
For a civilization in space: the ones that survive are the ones that stopped expanding, living quietly in comet shelters around long-lived stars.
For the Fermi paradox: that's why the sky looks empty. The survivors are quiet by design. They don't build empires or blaze signals across the galaxy; the loud, expansionist ones burn out. The universe isn't dead, it's full of retired civilizations we can't see.
It's a real position in the literature. Haqq-Misra and Baum called it the "sustainability solution" (2009), and Geoffrey Landis's percolation model makes a related point: expansion isn't the stable outcome.
The standard objection is that it only needs to fail once. A single expansionist civilization could fill a galaxy in a few tens of millions of years, which is short on cosmic timescales. Your series already has an answer: it's not a rule everyone obeys but a selection effect. Expanders fragment across light-years into rivals and strangers, and burn through their resources. The quiet ones are simply what's left.
Book III adds a nice twist. If surviving civilizations don't just hide but also seed simple life on suitable worlds, then the galaxy isn't only quietly inhabited, it's quietly gardened. That opens an optional, deliberately unanswerable question the series could brush against: was Earth's own life seeded by some older Ark, the way Haven's mining bacteria gave rise to her "children"? Left as a single line of wonder, never confirmed, it would tie your Fermi answer, the seeding strategy, and the trilogy's origin mystery into one loop."

## Second feedback — verbatim

"That's a great idea, and the science gives it a surprisingly good fit. One refinement makes it stronger: make it LUCA's ancestor, not LUCA itself.
LUCA, the Last Universal Common Ancestor, isn't the first life on Earth. It's the most recent organism that every living thing today descends from, and it was already sophisticated: a full genetic code, ribosomes, and, in one recent estimate, thousands of genes. Something simpler came before it. If that something arrived rather than arose, LUCA is simply where its lineage had gotten to by the time the family tree branched. So "a seeded ancestor of LUCA" is the version a biologist would accept as a fictional premise.
Why it fits your mining bacteria so well:
Early life may have literally eaten rock. Some reconstructions picture LUCA as a chemolithoautotroph living near hydrothermal vents, getting its energy from hydrogen and minerals rather than sunlight. Lithotroph means "rock-eater." Your Arks' mining bacteria, digesting minerals from comets and asteroids, are the same kind of organism.
Life appeared early. Earth formed about 4.5 billion years ago, and life seems to have shown up fast, with LUCA recently estimated at around 4.2 billion years. Some people find that speed a small puzzle.
It has real scientific precedent. In 1973 Francis Crick and Leslie Orgel proposed "directed panspermia," the idea that a civilization might have deliberately seeded Earth. It was a serious speculation, not a mainstream theory, and it remains unproven.
What it does for the series is close a loop: an older Ark's mining bacteria seed Earth, their descendants become humanity, humanity builds Arks with mining bacteria, and those bacteria give rise to Haven's "children." Life seeding life seeding life, with no clear first link. It echoes your earlier idea of realities with no in or out, and the ouroboros.
The one rule is the same as for the door: never confirm it. The mainstream view is that life began on Earth, and the book shouldn't claim otherwise. A single moment works best. Haven studies her children's biochemistry and finds something uncomfortably familiar, something that rhymes with the oldest records in her own Library, and she doesn't pursue it. Readers who know what LUCA is will feel the floor drop; everyone else will just feel a chill."
