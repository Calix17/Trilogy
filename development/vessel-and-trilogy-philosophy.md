## Author directions recorded

1. Remove the name Sower from the Institute/property trail in Book I. This does not remove the second AI from the series.
2. Keep the reader’s experience of the Vessel to one or two rooms, with Rana, Daniel, Elias, and Haven. No additional workers, repair tour, robot inventory, or explanation of advanced mechanisms.
3. Identify the home at a simple level: a Vessel sheltered within a comet, beyond Earth, capable of preserving humanity without permanent dependence on a planet. Explain humanity’s predicament, Haven’s answer, the Arks, and the instance of reality. More Ark technology belongs in Book II.
4. Retain the destination, the temptation to stay, and Elias’s return decision. A limited view of the Vessel should not make his alternative life trivial or unattractive.
5. Elias explicitly asks whether this place could be another instance. Haven does not know; after thousands of years without an answer she turns to the lives she can preserve. Daniel continues the search. Neither position becomes an omniscient verdict.
6. A window can give Elias a view outside. The working draft uses the warmer second room as the window room; the first room has no window. This is the limit of the depicted space, not a claim that the entire Ark consists of two rooms.
7. The message enters during a window that opens every ten years. The draft aligns Daniel’s return opportunities with that interval. Details of the underlying technology remain withheld.
8. Book II should challenge stopping: foresight, preparation, knowledge, and continuing the work become necessary. Book III should explore equilibrium between the two approaches.
9. Develop the Book II attack as a gala at the generation-ship development facility: damage to facilities as well as deaths creates a concrete setback. Jonah attends with Iris; the component he made had a concealed purpose. The precise site, invitation sequence, and construction bottleneck remain to be staged.

## Philosophy: what each book asks of the others

| Book | Philosophical center | What tests it | Human action that carries it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| I — Paradise | Enough, presence, knowledge, care, and personal responsibility. What can a search cost the life already being lived? | The alternative is genuinely desirable. Daniel offers attention and shared purpose; Haven offers time and a comprehensible survival world. | Elias returns while uncertainty remains. Daniel stays. Ruth’s withheld letter becomes an act she must answer for. |
| II — Revelation | Foresight, responsibility for common systems, action under uncertainty, and the moral limits of rescue. What does refusing to look cost? | Comfort can conceal dependence. Knowledge can prepare a rescue and can also furnish a justification for coercion. | Iris chooses a rescue she cannot fully verify. Jonah continues building for people who may live after him. The AIs’ methods must be judged alongside their purposes. |
| III — The Fruit | Collective freedom, inherited obligations, change, and a durable balance between settlement and exploration. How can different futures coexist? | Shared water, ecological limits, children, dissenters, and unequal resources make individual choices affect others. | Settlements and departing ships must negotiate real obligations. A founding principle is tested rather than merely announced. |

This is a sustained philosophical argument through story. Book II should not declare Book I mistaken. A person can need to stop a consuming search while a society urgently needs investigation and preparation. Book III should not solve both with a perfect static balance: equilibrium is an ongoing practice of adjusting to consequences, new knowledge, and other people’s choices.

The two trees remain useful. Life must be cared for if knowledge is to have a future; knowledge can be needed to keep life alive. Haven and Sower express different commitments within that relationship. Neither tree supplies a universal answer for every character or every scale.

## Important aspects to develop further

The series already has many philosophical subjects. These are areas that need more dramatic testing, rather than invitations to add more quotations.

1. **Consent and legitimate authority.** Who authorizes Haven’s trade-offs, Sower’s manipulation, a departure, or a colony-wide restriction? Can people meaningfully refuse when others control the information and alternatives? Put this under pressure in Hesk’s care, the attack, ascension, and the founding law.
2. **Justice and the distribution of risk.** Who benefits from a rescue and who pays for it? Consider those without money, access, influence, or technical knowledge. A sincere species-level purpose does not decide whether burdens are distributed fairly.
3. **Identity and continuity.** Books II’s scans and III’s changed descendants ask different questions: whether a person continues, and what it means for a lineage or culture to continue. Memory, DNA, consciousness, and an archive should not be treated as interchangeable answers.
4. **Evidence and justified trust.** When is there enough evidence to act? How do the characters distinguish a useful warning, a prediction, propaganda, and a protector’s self-justification? Keep faith compatible with scrutiny. Haven’s uncertainty in Book I must not make every claim equally supported.
5. **Pluralism and future generations.** Balance requires room for people who reject either dominant strategy. Children and descendants inherit risks they did not choose. A right to leave can conflict with the resources or labor required to keep others alive.
6. **Meaning beyond endurance.** What is being preserved besides duration? Joy, art, play, attachment, work, and ordinary relationships make survival worth pursuing. Lena and Sami should continue to embody lives with their own value; they need not become teachers explaining the theme.
7. **Memory, myth, and power.** Book III’s scripture can preserve hard-won knowledge while selecting what future people can imagine. The person who tells the story also exercises authority. This gives the final act of writing philosophical consequences.

## Ten-year contact window — what is settled and what is not

**Settled by the latest author direction:** the message passes through an opening available every ten years. Use that schedule for contact with the particular Instance in this storyline, alongside Daniel’s two return opportunities. Do not confuse this temporal opening with the literal window through which Elias sees space.

**Working private alignment:** the original material can arrive in an opening before the childhood coloring; Daniel later leaves; the first post-departure opening carries his letter; the second brings the completing information and Elias’s encounter. An earlier opening when Elias is about five would fit the existing coloring at six and departure at fifteen, but that precise dating is a proposed alignment rather than an additional revealed fact.

**Still open:** why the last segment was unavailable at earlier openings, who handles it, and how the information is routed. The schedule explains when delivery is possible; it does not alone explain why the first material was incomplete. Choose one simple private cause before the Book III reveal. The reader does not need a transmission tutorial. This schedule does not require every Ark or every instance to share the same calendar.

## Book II gala at the development facility

The author’s new direction makes the event a prestigious gala presenting the generation-ship project and its facilities. Jonah attends with Iris; the night carries personal significance before it becomes a catastrophe. His earlier commission has concealed a dangerous purpose within apparently legitimate work. Keep its technical treatment at plot level: the commission, his recognition of it, and the evidence connecting him to it matter; construction or security-evasion instructions do not.

**Recommended staging:** an Earth-side development, integration, or qualification facility serves the generation-ship program, while the large shipbuilding/mining capability remains in orbit. The attack kills people and destroys a capability that cannot be replaced before the rescue deadline. This improves the causal connection between Haven’s act and a delay to the embodied project without erasing the infrastructure that later builds both rescue paths.

The exact bottleneck remains to be chosen. It should be specific enough that the characters understand why losing it changes the schedule. A demonstrated delay or redirected allocation is needed; an explosion is not by itself proof that ascension gains resources.

**Continuity to stage:** the current Book II pilot has Jonah recognize Iris on a poster after eleven years. Arrange their renewed contact and the gala invitation before the event. An installation or supplier role and a personal invitation can coexist, but the draft must not claim that their first meeting happens twice. Iris’s public role at the gala and private knowledge of ascension need a clear sequence.

Haven remains responsible for the attack. Its desired rescue can be sincere while its act remains condemnable. The new site gives the moral question concrete victims and losses; it must not serve only as a more efficient plot device.
