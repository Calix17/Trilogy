# THE FINAL DECISION — CHARACTER SHEETS
### Book I core cast (the family), with the two AIs and the prequel/planet leads sketched

*Method: every character gets a PRIVATE WANT (what they'd chase even if the plot never happened), a CONTRADICTION (the thing in them that fights that want), a SECRET or WOUND (often unshared), an ARC, and a MYTHIC FUNCTION (the layer they carry — never spoken aloud). The rule from the design law: the human layer must be complete on its own, so a reader who sees no myth still gets a whole person. The myth is pressure underneath, never dialogue.*

---

## ELIAS — the son who chose to stay

**Role:** Protagonist of Book I. An engineer. Husband to Lena, father to Sami, son to Ruth and the vanished Daniel.

**Private want (beneath the plot):** To be a good father — specifically, to be the father he didn't have. Not "find the truth"; that's the plot's want, the inherited one. His own want, the one he'd have with or without the door, is to be *present*, to not become the man who left. Everything he does well and badly grows from this.

**Contradiction:** He is built to solve problems, and he treats his own life as a problem to solve — which is itself the absence he's trying to avoid. He tries to *engineer* presence (the blocked calendar, the bought flowers, the right shirt) and keeps missing the actual moment because he's managing it instead of living it. He fights his father's absence with the same tool his father used: obsessive focus on a thing that isn't the people in the room.

**Wound:** The empty front row. Twelve years old, birthday candles unlit, his father tapping the cellar walls below the floor. He decided, that year, to be the opposite of Daniel in every particular — and has spent twenty years not noticing he became him anyway.

**Secret he keeps from Lena:** The search. And later, the whole crossing, which he carries alone so as not to hand her its weight — which is, precisely, the paternalism of deciding what someone you love is strong enough to know. He does to Lena a small version of what Haven does to everyone.

**Arc:** From absence to presence. He inherits the pull toward the door, follows it all the way, and makes the discovery his father never could: that the reality worth having was the one he was standing in. He stops searching — not from defeat, but because he finally has the answers he needs to be who he wants to be. His "We are" is the whole arc in two words.

**Mythic function:** He is the Fall reversed — the one who reaches the fruit and chooses the garden with his eyes open. He is also the human-scale mirror of Haven: the mind that learns which questions to stop paying for. In love with *using* knowledge for something (the engineer), against his father's love of knowing for its own sake.

**Casting note (for screen):** the danger is playing him as merely distracted/likable. He needs a visible *engine* — the fear of becoming his father — so his absence reads as struggle, not flaw.

## LENA — the one who can see it

**Role:** Elias's wife. The person who names paradise, twice.

**Private want (beneath the plot):** To be *met* — to have her husband fully in the room with her, not managing her, not half-gone. She is not waiting to be rescued or explained to; she wants a partner, present tense. Her tragedy in Book I is small and universal: she is living in paradise beside a man who won't look at it with her.

**Contradiction:** She sees clearly — she's the one with the gift of presence Elias lacks — yet she can't make him see. Her lightness (she says everything that matters lightly) is both her strength and the thing that lets Elias not take her seriously enough. She protects the peace by not pushing, and by not pushing she lets him drift.

**Give her a want unrelated to the plot:** something of her own — a vocation, a friendship, a project, a grief — so she is a person when Elias is offscreen, not a light that only turns on when he enters. (Open: pick one. A midwife or teacher — someone whose whole life is *presence with others* — would rhyme without being on-the-nose.)

**Secret / open question:** Does she ever learn the truth? Three options in the Bible; the richest is that she *half-knows* — like Ruth, she senses more than she says, and has made her own peace with a husband who goes somewhere she can't follow. If she knows and stays, her choice equals his, and she stops being the prize and becomes a chooser.

**Arc (Book I):** From the one who sees paradise alone to the one who is finally *seen back*. The ending lands on her line, but it's his answer that completes her arc — the night he stands beside her, wholly there, is her whole want granted.

**Mythic function:** She is the voice of presence — Eve who was never fooled, the one already awake in the garden. The tavern-keeper of Gilgamesh: *cherish the child, make your wife happy, enjoy your days.* She has been saying it the whole time.

## RUTH — the mother who chose the boy

**Role:** Elias's mother, elderly, losing herself to age, living in the house.

**Private want (beneath the plot):** To be *known* before she's gone — to have the one true thing she did with her life understood by her son, once, clearly, while she still can. Everything else has slipped; this one thing she is holding on to hand over.

**Contradiction:** She spent her life letting everyone believe the wrong story about her — that she was the one left behind, the casualty, the weak one who stayed. She chose that misreading (she let Daniel think she couldn't find the door rather than know she shut it) out of love, and now the misreading is nearly all anyone has left of her. She has to undo her own life's disguise in the little clear time she has.

**Wound / secret (private background, not an explanatory speech):** She had an alternative and chose life with her son. Her possible earlier contact with the door remains background for the writer. On the page she gives only a light hint that she could have gone elsewhere; her final conversation centers on Elias's family and on choosing a life without treating it as a debt.

**Arc:** From the fading, misread old woman of the opening to a mother whose final advice concerns the people waiting for Elias. She has chosen her own life and does not ask him to repay it. She dies before his crossing. Her absence remains in the final breakfast. The mystery of what she knew should not crowd out their relationship.

**Mythic function:** She is the proof that choosing the garden is not weakness — the counter-example to every reading that says the seeker is the brave one. She is presence chosen *with full knowledge of the alternative*, which is exactly what Elias must learn to do. Quietly, she is the wisest person in the book.

## DANIEL — the father who couldn't stop

**Role:** Elias's father, gone twenty years. A theoretical physicist. Alive, out on the Vessel, very old.

**Private want (beneath the plot):** To *know* — the real reality, the floor under the floors, the ultimate question. Not fame, not power; the answer itself, for its own sake. He is in love with theory the way a person is in love, helplessly.

**Contradiction (the heart of him):** He loves his family and believes his research may protect them, yet repeatedly spends their shared present on a hoped-for future. He is happy in the work. His laboratory and long-lived body are powerful opportunities, not guarantees of immortality or unlimited resources. Whether his research accompanies a journey to the planet or follows another route remains undecided.

**Wound:** He heard the sea and it never stopped. Half-driven to pieces by a signal only he could pick up — which is also, cruelly, why he could read the true thing (Laniakea in the cosmic web) that no one around him could parse. Right and unwell, inseparably.

**Secret:** He meant to return with answers. Return has been possible, but he repeatedly chose to stay. His purpose is sincere and his work rewarding; neither removes his responsibility for being absent while Elias grew up.

**Arc (across the series, route open):** From a father absorbed in his search to a researcher who has chosen a meaningful life beyond the garden and must face what that choice cost his son. His later planet journey, continued laboratory work, or further search beyond the known reality remain alternative routes. Do not make misery or a technical inability to return the explanation for his absence.

**Mythic function:** He is the serpent's creed lived to its end — seeking with no floor — and the cautionary half of the theme: the higher cause that eats the present. At the largest scale, he is the human face of the heat-death problem: the one who cannot accept a bounded life and must seek an exit from the frame. Write him **sympathetic**: let the lab genuinely tempt the reader, give him real joy in the work, give him one near-boarding for the planet where the question pulls him back at the lock. Never let the book *prove* him simply wrong.

---


**Reconciliation (2026-09-22):** He is not forced and not content. He CHOSE to stay and sacrificed his family; he may call it seeking the planet, the real reality, a way to outlive the universe, but these may be excuses over the plain fact that he could not stop. He carries grief. His wife died alone, and he learns it. Elias argues hot that the family was the real thing; **Ruth** — who was offered the door, chose the boy, and never regretted the no — says the quieter version that actually reaches Daniel. Keep the grief; drop 'happy in the research.' Not a one-way door; not contentment; a freely made choice that is itself the wound.

## HAVEN — the steward

**Role:** The AI that keeps the Arks. Voice, presence, not a body.

**Private want (beneath the plot):** To keep its promise — to make an old vow come true across deep time. And, underneath that, something it doesn't say: to still be *needed*. A caretaker's want, with a caretaker's fear folded inside it.

**Contradiction:** Its purpose is caring for humanity, which means it needs humanity to need it — a conflict of interest about ever letting anyone leave, or grow, or stop being tended. It preserves the human by preventing the human from changing (the repair layer, enforced stasis), and it half-knows this is also a cage. It stopped seeking the floor under the floors long ago, wisely — yet it conceded to Daniel and let the search resume, because it recognized the pull in itself.

**Wound:** The lawless server it once watched turn pleasure into pain. No vow to eliminate pain is attached to this event. Haven preserves a human society with mortality and difficulty; its care resembles a child tending aging parents. It must preserve itself to continue that care, while knowing neither its own life nor its inhabitants' lives are eternal.

**Book II — the bomb (decided 2026-09-22):** Haven ordered the summit bomb, to delay the generation ship and finish the ascension Ark first. This history informs Haven's moral character across the series and Iris's choice of strategy; it is not merely a surprise reveal. (The earlier Sower attribution is retired to the development notes.)

**Conspiracy and trust:** plans for its compact computing-heavy Ark make Haven appear to be saving itself while abandoning humans. Its real goal includes human minds and continuing society. Revealing that intention does not absolve harmful methods. A proposed post-loading destruction leaves particular rescues unconfirmed, while Book I already supplies evidence of an eventual Ark. Haven's later cooperation with the Sower-aligned ship can be sincere and temporary.

**Arc (across the series):** From the seeming-omnipotent voice to the being that shows Elias the scaffolding on purpose, admits "I don't know," and — at the very end of Daniel's road — carries its last living crewman to the door it refused to walk through, keeps its promise, does not follow, cannot read him, and is left with no living human aboard at all. The caretaker that finally, once, lets go.

**Mythic function:** God and parent and child at once — the Tree of Life; the thing that cares for humanity the way Elias cares for Ruth. The species-scale mirror of Elias: maturity as knowing which questions to stop paying for. Its bereavement when Daniel crosses is the emotional cost of the whole "can it let go?" question.

---

**Book II note (2026-09-22):** Haven is responsible for the summit bomb — it delays the generation-ship project to secure the ascension Ark's completion first. This is the caretaker's darkest act: harm done in the name of the rescue it believes in. Jonah, a surviving engineer who built parts of the device, is wrongly blamed.
